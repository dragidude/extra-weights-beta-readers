The Delay

Elena Vargas’s helicopter lifted off without Bob.

Rotor wash drove loose snow across the black gravel. Bob turned his face away until the aircraft cleared the pad, then watched it bank toward the mountains. His own flight should have left McMurdo the day before. Instead, he was standing beside Sofia Alvarez, one of his field crew, watching another expedition leave.

“We can still get out today,” Sofia said when the noise had faded. “It’s only a day, Bob. We have weeks.”

“I’d rather spend them there.”

“So would I.”

He checked his watch, caught himself, and lowered his arm. Sofia was right. A day should not decide the success of a field season. It would still give Vargas time to reach Lake Bonney, unload, and get her rovers moving toward Taylor Glacier while Bob inspected cargo pallets in McMurdo. His research sponsor, SAGE, had found a reason to hold him here. It had not found a reason it could share.

Things weren’t going Bob’s way.

The problem wasn’t Vargas. He had mentored her in his lab years ago, and arguing with her was still one of the pleasures of his profession. Conference hallways, coffee shops—anywhere they met could become an argument. Vargas would say something irritatingly clever, Bob would tell her why it was wrong, and somewhere in the exchange one or both of them would learn something. He wanted her to do well. He also wanted to get into the field first.

The problem was that her research was waxing just as his waned. Students who once might have crowded into his lab now wanted to work with Vargas. Grants and conference buzz followed.

His own work still mattered; people cited it all the time. Recently they had started calling it foundational.

Foundational.

To Bob it meant: thank you for your service, that’ll be all. He had become an extinct volcano: still on the map, historically significant, no longer expected to erupt. Taylor might be his last chance to change that.

He had spent much of his career asking a deceptively simple question about evolution. Before natural selection favored one genetic change over another, which changes did it have to choose from? Cells did not generate every mutation equally often. The machinery that copied and repaired their DNA made some changes more likely than others, and stress or a change in chemistry could shift those odds again. No cell needed to know which mutation would help. Some kinds simply got more chances to appear—more shots on goal, but not from everywhere equally.

The idea had a name: mutation bias. Bob had built his research around it. The familiar peppered moth showed why a successful change was only part of the story. Near smoky factories, dark moths had spread because birds were less likely to spot them on soot-darkened trees. A mutation had supplied the dark form; selection favored it. The dark moths still developed six legs, following the same inherited body plan as the pale ones. Knowing why a useful mutation spread did not tell Bob how likely it had been to appear in the first place.

At Taylor, he hoped to catch those odds changing in microbial populations, before the successful variants multiplied and obscured what had happened first. He wanted to bring back something new, not another reason for people to admire his earlier work.

Vargas’s helicopter disappeared over the ridge. Bob stayed until he could no longer hear it, then followed Sofia back to the cargo.

Coring tools, drones, climbing gear, cold storage, batteries, shelters, sampling equipment: everything had been weighed, tested, packed, and checked. He opened the list and worked his way down it until he reached the cold-storage unit. Its panel showed four green lights. The temperature was steady, the backup battery was full, and the seal test had passed.

“You’ve checked that twice this morning,” Sofia said.

Bob had already put a hand on the lid. He took it off and closed the inspection list.

Everything was ready except the Class 3 scientific agent—the AI research assistant he had requested for the field work. SAGE had frozen its shipment under restricted review just hours before his scheduled departure. Because the agent was part of the mission authorization, the hold applied to Bob’s whole deployment. Leaving without it was not a decision he was allowed to make.

Inside SAGE, employees had a name for this kind of bureaucratic limbo: being black-boxed. A request remained visible while its owner, the reason for the hold, and the person who could lift it disappeared behind restricted fields. Bob had heard about a colleague who spent weeks trying to break one open, only to trigger another restricted layer hiding who had imposed the first. It had been funnier when it happened to someone else.

What made this particular hold so hard to understand was the agent he had chosen. Threes were reliable workhorses in labs and field operations, not experimental systems that needed a last-minute review. Back at his university, his lab’s in-house Three, Charlie, had stopped a run a few weeks earlier because two labeling rules conflicted. Charlie showed Bob the problem and waited. Bob decided which rule took precedence, amended the instructions, and had him working again in under five minutes.

That was the sort of interruption Bob could live with. Charlie could run instruments, track samples, build basic models, and chew through repetitive analysis that would bury a human team. At the edge of his instructions, he stopped and asked. Sometimes the interruption was annoying, but Bob knew what had caused it and what he needed to do.

Over the years he had accumulated thousands of rules for sample handling, contamination responses, escalation, and exceptions to escalation, until Charlie’s library had become indispensable. Other researchers, mostly older ones, still requested copies. The shared library carried a 4.8-out-of-5 rating.

Class 4 agents were newer and more autonomous. Adapting Bob’s field-rule system to use them would have taken years, and he already had a system that worked. For Taylor he had requested another Three, with the same field-rule package and the same familiar limits. He had chosen the predictable option. SAGE had somehow made it the one thing he could not plan around.

He stepped away from the cold-storage unit and called Eli Park, his junior program manager at SAGE. They had barely spoken before yesterday’s call about the hold. Since then, Eli had become the person Bob spoke to most often.

“Eli, you have to help. What’s blocking my Class 3?”

“I still can’t see who owns the review.”

“Can you release the approved unit while they finish it?”

“I tried. The hold won’t let me.”

Bob put the call on speaker and opened the authorization. “Then separate the agent from the rest of the mission. Let us get our equipment into the field.”

“That was refused too.”

Eli went through the other route he had tried: substituting a different certified unit. That could not be arranged in a useful timeframe. Bob followed the references in the authorization until he reached the same restricted field Eli had.

“What else?”

“I’m working every contact I have.”

Bob drew breath to ask which contacts, then looked again at the requests Eli had sent. Each was something Bob would have tried himself. Eli was not the person keeping him here. He was Bob’s only reachable ally inside SAGE.

“I’m sorry,” Bob said. “I know this isn’t your fault. You’re doing good work.”

“You’re fine. Keep pushing me. We’re going to fix this.”

“Okay. Keep digging.”

Later that morning, Eli managed to squeeze one more fact from the black box: a Special Programs code was attached to the review. It gave Bob a place to look, but no one he could ask through the official channel.

He was looking through the authorization again when Sofia came over with a field update from Vargas and her Class 4 agent, Echo.

“Look at the second image,” she said.

Bob opened the first to orient himself. Taylor Glacier lay west of Lake Bonney. Near its margin, a stretch of ice, frozen sediment, and gravel formed the best route for their small rovers and science trailers. He knew the map well enough to recognize where the second image differed. A water outlet shown on every recent survey had almost stopped flowing. Farther downhill, Echo’s radar and thermal passes showed water spreading across ground that had been dry the day before.

The water had found another way through.

This was why Bob had wanted to work at Taylor. Ice and permafrost had kept pockets of salty water—brine—and the microbes living in them apart for different lengths of time, sometimes millions of years. The surrounding sediment and fractured rock helped make each pocket a different environment, with its own chemistry and biological history. When meltwater connected two of them, organisms that had lived under one set of conditions encountered another. The new chemistry could change how DNA was damaged and repaired, and which genetic changes appeared.

Bob wanted material from both sides before they mixed, then samples from the place where the waters met. He could compare the earliest states he could reach with what followed. But once the water carried organisms and chemistry from one pocket into another, he could no longer be sure where everything had started. The event he wanted to study was already happening, and each hour of it could take part of the comparison away.

Sofia added the route overlay. The projected water path crossed beneath the ground they planned to use for the trailers.

Bob leaned closer and traced the route with a finger. There was no obvious sag or open crack. The measurements could not tell him how much material still supported the surface. He could not declare the route lost on that evidence, but he would not send a loaded trailer across it on the strength of yesterday’s map either.

Moving water could hollow out ice or cut through frozen sediment while the surface still looked solid. A person might cross a weak section and never know it. A science trailer could put a wheel through the same spot.

Without rover access, they could still carry light gear to parts of Taylor, fill bottles, and come home with interesting samples. What they could not carry on their backs was the full set of drills, batteries, cold storage, and equipment for handling material without exposing it to air. They needed that equipment to keep samples close enough to their original conditions to measure what had actually been there.

Vargas’s revised survey plan arrived while they were studying the route. She had moved her next survey away from the projected flow and marked the suspect ground for repeated checks. Bob read the changes and nodded. She was doing what he would have done: protecting access without wasting the day waiting for the glacier to become predictable.

Sofia left the route on the display and brought up their cargo list beside it.

Bob looked from the map to the pallets. Losing a day to Vargas was one thing. Arriving after Taylor had mixed the samples or closed the heavy route was another. He could not make SAGE release the shipment, but he could make sure the right equipment left first when it did.

“We’d better adjust our first load.”

“What moves up?”

“The preservation gear. Oxygen-free kits, sterile sampling equipment, cold storage. Keep enough power to preserve samples if the main camp package gets delayed. Everything else can argue for a seat.”

For the next two hours, Sofia revised the cargo list while Bob tested the new sequence against the routes. Lower-priority analytical equipment moved to later loads. They checked alternate approaches and allowed for another change in the drainage before they arrived. Sofia checked that the preservation gear could work on its own, without waiting for the rest of the camp equipment to arrive.

Sofia paused over the secondary freezer. “Do you still want both on the first load?”

Bob checked the weight they had freed, then looked at the two freezer entries.

“Yes. If we find a sample worth keeping, I don’t want to pick what gets left behind.”

The freezer stayed. Even with it, the first load was lighter, and the sample-preservation gear would arrive earlier. Bob checked the totals once more. This time there was a difference worth checking.

By noon they had changed everything they could justify changing. Sofia saved the revised list, and Bob looked out across the pallets. After two hours of useful work, every one of them was still on the cargo floor.

Eli had no further answer. The Special Programs code was still the only clue to who might be able to act, and it pointed to someone Bob knew.

He found Nathan Briggs in his contacts and stopped with his thumb over the name. Then he locked the phone and set it down.

Briggs was SAGE’s Deputy Director for Special Programs. Years ago, as Colonel Briggs, he had recruited Bob into extreme-environment field work and taught him a rule Bob still used: as simple as possible, but not too simple. Briggs would take one of Bob’s elaborate plans, cross out half the contingencies, and ask which remaining variable actually changed the decision. Bob had built his approach to field work around that question.

He picked the phone up again. Usually, by the time he called Briggs, he could say what the problem was and what he wanted to do about it. Today he could not even identify the person holding his expedition. It felt like bringing an unfinished problem back to the teacher who had taught him to solve it himself.

He looked at the changed route on the display. He disliked making this call. He disliked the prospect of losing Taylor more.

Bob unlocked the phone and called.

The secure line rang without an answer. He tried again, let it ring longer, and left a message explaining the hold and the new drainage. Then he went back to the cargo floor with the phone in his hand.

He waited a while, checking his messages too often and finding no reply from Briggs. Apparently Briggs was black-boxing him too.

He put the phone away and stopped in front of the cold-storage unit. Four green lights, temperature steady, battery full. His hand went toward the lid.

Sofia looked at Bob, then at the unit. She did not say a word.

“I’m just checking the seal.”

She looked back at him and waited.

“Okay, okay.”

He let his hand fall and handed her the inspection list. “I’ll be in the gym.”

Sofia nodded and went back to work.

McMurdo’s gym was a converted room with rubber flooring, a rack, a bench, and an old bar that was straight enough to trust. Bob changed, set his phone where he could hear it, and began warming up. At sixty-one, he added weight carefully and gave himself time between sets.

He had once placed second in masters powerlifting and still trained toward the numbers that had put him there. The bar moved well enough that he loaded it for one heavy deadlift he expected to make.

He set his feet, gripped the bar, braced, and pulled. The plates rose from the floor. At his shins the bar began to slow, and below his knees it stopped.

Bob pulled harder. His back and legs tightened against the weight, but the bar did not move. He held the strain for another second, then let it fall. The plates crashed against the floor. He stood over them, breathing hard, and waited until he could take a full breath without fighting for it.

He took twenty kilograms off and set himself again. This time the bar kept moving past his shins and knees until he stood upright with it.

Better.

He dropped the bar to the floor and finished the workout with lighter sets and measured rests. He did not put the twenty kilograms back on. By the time he finished, the expedition was no closer to leaving, but he was no longer reaching for his phone between every set.

Sofia found him while he was pulling on his jacket.

“SAGE released the transfer.”

Bob stopped with one arm in a sleeve. “The Three?”

“The contents are still restricted. There’s a revised manifest.”

She handed him the display. The SAGE shipment was four hundred twenty kilograms heavier than the system he had ordered.

“Four hundred twenty?”

“I had the same question.”

Bob checked the packaging weight, then the units, then the total against his original order. There was no error he could find.

“What’s the item?”

“Restricted.”

“Who approved it?”

“Restricted.”

Nearly half a ton had appeared inside his expedition. It would change the aircraft loads, the balance of the trailers, and what other equipment could travel with it. He had spent the morning making room for the science gear. Now he had to find room for something he was not allowed to identify.

A low engine rumble came through the wall. Bob finished pulling on his jacket and followed Sofia outside. Beyond the freight sheds, a transfer tractor rounded the berm from the airfield, towing the SAGE shipment toward the loading apron.

SAGE had not answered Bob’s question. Instead, it had delivered him a four-hundred-twenty-kilogram question.
